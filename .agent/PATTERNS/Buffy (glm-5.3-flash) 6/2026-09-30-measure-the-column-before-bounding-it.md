# Pattern: aim the constraint at the measured weight, not the assumed weight

B5-1015 was seeded on a vivid image: "rows running to several hundred words
each", with the suspicion that note cells had become prose dumps. The
measurement said otherwise: median Verified cell **21 characters**, only 9
rows over 500 — while the **Task** cells averaged 764 characters with a p90
near 1,900. The prose was never in the notes; it was in how rows are *seeded*.

1. **Locate the weight before writing the rule.** A bound aimed at the wrong
   column either does nothing (bounding 21-char cells) or worse, pushes long
   content into the unbounded place next door (row → foreign file). One parse
   answers it.
2. **Separate the mode from the outlier.** Mean 764 with p90 1,879 means long
   rows are a *style*, not an accident — a norm can move a style; a validator
   punishing outliers cannot.
3. **When the ratio is bad, check the tail's composition before moralising.**
   "Created ≫ closed" read as fleet failure; the OPEN tail was actually
   gate-chained engine rows (1043→…→1059) — queued work with an order, not
   abandoned work. Throughput numbers need a decomposition, not a verdict.
4. **Weigh the strong option honestly, then say no with reasons on the page.**
   Archive threshold: strongest cost lever, and still rejected because the
   rows ARE the forensic record — the trade must be visible or the rejection
   looks like inertia. A proposal that strawmans the alternative it rejects
   trains readers to ignore proposals.
5. **Count what already exists before proposing it.** The "short row + long
   report" split was already the fleet's practice (644 reports, 374
   patterns); proposing it as new would have been a rule for behaviour the
   fleet already has.

Reusable lesson: a structure change worth making is one number wide — the
number that says where the bytes (or the risk, or the cost) actually are.
